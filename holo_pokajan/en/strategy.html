Strategy & Safe Tiles

Pokajan is a balancing act between "aggressively winning to rack up hands" and "playing defense to avoid dealing in." Here we break down how to play each phase of the game and how to identify safe tiles.

Core Idea

Core Approach: Win Aggressively, Even on Cheap Hands

✅

Passing up a win costs you: the basic approach is to declare as soon as any yaku is complete, even the cheapest 120-point hand. Reports suggest that holding out for bigger yaku tends to actually lower your average score over time. Since your hand refills back up to 7 cards after every Pokajan, "winning small, often" adds up into a big overall difference.

⚠️

Over-defending backfires too: trying to avoid every dangerous tile leaves you stuck with a dead hand, which ends up costing you more opportunities than it saves. The optimal approach is a "moderate defense" — avoiding just one or two of the riskiest-looking tiles rather than everything.

Group Focus

Narrowing Down Groups (Early Game)

Narrowing your "target groups" down to about 2–3 early in the round keeps your card-keeping decisions consistent for the rest of the match.

👍 Groups worth prioritizing

Smaller groups, like 3-member units, tend to be easier to complete and more practical in real play. Groups that include a bonus Holomen also move up in priority since you can stack extra points on top.

👎 Groups worth deprioritizing

Large groups, like 5-member units, pay out a lot but come with a much higher bar to clear. Spreading yourself thin by chasing several of these early on tends to result in completing none of them. Learning to cut your losses early and say "not this round" on a group is important too.

🔄

Don't treat your narrowed-down groups as fixed — stay flexible and switch your target based on what's happening at the table (who's discarding what). Cards that work across multiple yaku (like the same Holomen who's also part of your target group) are worth prioritizing to keep.

Phase

Play by Phase

  1. 1

    Early game: prioritize speed

    Narrow your target yaku down to 1–2, and rack up wins from cheap, easy-to-complete hands to keep your hand cycling. Favor offense over defense at this stage.

  2. 2

    Mid game: switch offense/defense based on your rank

    If you're near the top, don't push your luck — shift toward prioritizing safe tiles. If you're behind, consider switching toward higher-value yaku. Read the discards on the table to guess which groups other players are chasing, and treat those as dangerous.

  3. 3

    Late game: protect your placement by avoiding big deal-ins

    Once the deck drops under 20 cards, other players' hands become easier to read. If you've secured 1st or 2nd place, make avoiding a big deal-in your top priority. Conversely, if you're way behind, you may need to switch gears and go for a high-value comeback hand.

    Especially when you're winning, it's often better to discard cards tied to lower-value yaku (like 3-member group units) first, accepting a small deal-in as the worst-case scenario, rather than risk a much bigger one.

Safe Tiles

Spotting Safe vs. Dangerous Tiles

🛡️ Cards that tend to be safer

・The same card another player just discarded (matching a recent discard)
・Cards that have already appeared multiple times in discard piles
・A card discarded by the player right before your turn
・Cards where most other members of that group have already hit the field (making the group yaku unlikely)
・Holomen whose matching-color cards are largely exhausted for the round (making a same-color bonus unlikely)

💣 Cards that tend to be more dangerous

・Cards that haven't appeared on the field at all yet (another player may be holding them)
・Cards from a Holomen/group a specific player keeps discarding — they may actually be hoarding related cards behind the scenes
・Cards from the designated bonus Holomen (highly sought-after, and costly if you deal in)

🎯

Matching a recent discard is your basic defense: discarding the same card that was just discarded — sometimes called a "safety match" — is the most fundamental way to avoid dealing in. The logic: if that card could complete a hand, the player right before you would have already won off it instead of passing.

*"Expected copies" is the theoretical average number of that character's (or color's) cards you'd expect within 100 cards in play (a hypergeometric expectation: 100 × matching cards ÷ total cards). Actual results will vary round to round.

💡

The bigger the roster, the harder "same-color" gets: as the table shows, once the roster hits 18 characters, the expected copies per character-and-color drops to just 1.85. On average, that's fewer than 2 copies in play at all — meaning both you and your opponents will have a much harder time collecting the 3 needed for a same-color yaku.

🛡️

Applying this in practice: bigger rosters make "safe discards" easier to find: in a smaller roster environment (around 14 characters), each color is relatively easy to collect at 2.38 copies, so watching out for same-color yaku is worthwhile. In a larger roster environment (17–18 characters), the pool of cards needed for a same-color hand is simply too thin, so confidently discarding "same character, same color" cards as safe tiles becomes a viable play. As the roster keeps growing with future updates, keeping this "harder to complete" factor in the back of your mind should sharpen your tile-reading.

Data Worth Knowing

Some general trends we've observed through actual play. Use these as reference for your own strategy.

🎲 Starting hand matters less than you'd think

The link between a good starting hand and your final score is weaker than expected — your decisions after the deal matter more than the luck of your initial draw.

💺 Seating position barely matters

There's little to no noticeable advantage or disadvantage from where you're seated, so it doesn't seem worth worrying about.

📈 Luck dominates short sessions

Even against beginners, you can lose more often than not over a short stretch of games — real skill differences only become clear after playing a fair number of matches. Don't let any single result get you too high or too low.
